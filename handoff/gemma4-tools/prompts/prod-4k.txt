Summarize the following field notes about tides in two sentences.

The sea rises and falls twice a day along most coasts, and the cause is the pull of the moon. The moon draws the water on the near side of the planet toward itself, which heaps the ocean into a low bulge. On the far side the pull is weaker than at the center of the planet, so the water there is left behind and forms a second bulge. As the planet turns beneath these two bulges, a harbor passes through high water, then low water, then high water again in a little more than a day. The sun raises tides as well, though less than half as strongly, because it is so much farther away. When the sun, the moon and the planet stand in a line, at new moon and at full moon, the two pulls add together and the range between high and low water is large. Sailors call these spring tides, although they have nothing to do with the season. A week later the sun and the moon pull at right angles, the range is small, and the tides are called neap tides. The shape of the coast matters as much as the sky. In the open ocean the rise is less than a meter, but a funnel-shaped bay can squeeze the same wave of water until it climbs more than ten meters. Some river mouths are narrow enough that the incoming tide arrives as a single steep wave, a bore, that travels upstream against the current. Other seas are nearly closed off from the ocean and have almost no tide at all. People who work on the shore plan around these rhythms. A fishing boat that settles on the mud at low water cannot leave until the sea returns. A pilot bringing a deep ship into port waits for the flood so that there is enough water over the bar. Printed tables give the times and heights for each harbor years in advance, because the motions of the moon and the sun can be calculated long before they happen. Wind and air pressure still change the level on the day, so a careful mariner reads the table and then looks at the water.
